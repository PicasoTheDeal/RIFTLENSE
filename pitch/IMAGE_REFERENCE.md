# RIFTLENS image bank, page by page

This goes with RIFTLENS_images.pdf in the pitch folder. One page per image, 23 pages. For each one I wrote what you're actually looking at, why it's in our story, and where I'd put it. Credits and source links are printed on each PDF page and in pitch/images/CREDITS.md. None of these are AI generated.

## Page 1. dallol_1.jpg
What it is: the Dallol hydrothermal field in Afar. Yellow and green sulphur and salt deposits, acid ponds, little chimneys where hot brine comes up. Shot by A. Savin in 2018.
Why it matters: this is our hero image for the analog claim. Dallol is one of the most extreme places on Earth, acidic, hypersaline, hot, and astrobiologists study it to understand how life might have survived in Mars' ancient hydrothermal systems. If a judge only remembers one picture, it should be this one.
Where: slide 1 or slide 4 (analog scorecard demo).

## Page 2. dallol_2.jpg
What it is: Gaet'ale pond, a small hypersaline hot spring lake at Dallol. Same photographer.
Why it matters: a calmer companion to page 1. Shows the water and brine angle of the analog, which connects to the "water, ice and other resources" dimension NASA lists in the challenge.
Where: slide 4, or as background for the scorecard panel.

## Page 3. danakil_sentinel2_1.jpg
What it is: the Danakil Depression seen from orbit by ESA's Sentinel-2 satellite. Salt flats, the Dallol dome, Lake Karum, the volcanic range on the east side.
Why it matters: this is the "from space" view of the same place as pages 1 and 2. It shows the judges that the analog is visible at the scale satellites work at, which is the scale our scoring engine runs at.
Where: slide 4, or slide 3 when explaining the three layers.

## Page 4. danakil_map_1.jpg
What it is: a three panel scientific map of the Danakil Depression. Panel A is topography, B is regional context with the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, C is a satellite image.
Why it matters: it orients people who have never heard of Danakil. It also shows the depression sits below sea level between two spreading plates, which is the rifting story.
Where: slide 2 or 3, when setting up where Afar is.

## Page 5. erta_ale_1.jpg
What it is: the lava lake inside Erta Ale volcano in 2001. Glowing cracks in a dark crust.
Why it matters: Erta Ale had one of the only permanent lava lakes on the planet. In the fire data it shows up as a hotspot every single night, year after year. Our fire layer has to recognise that as a volcano and not count it as a wildfire. This image explains that problem in one look.
Where: slide 5 (fire layer demo).

## Page 6. erta_ale_2.jpg
What it is: the same lava lake in 2008, photographed from the crater rim, portrait orientation.
Why it matters: backup for page 5, works better in a vertical layout or as a side panel.
Where: slide 5 alternate.

## Page 7. fentale_1.jpg
What it is: Mount Fentale in Oromia, the volcano near Metehara on the Addis to Djibouti road. Green slopes with low cloud.
Why it matters: this is the volcano under the 2024 to 2025 earthquake swarm. The magma intrusion that cracked the road and railway ran from here toward Dofan. Judges will recognise the name.
Where: slide 2 (the problem) or slide 5 (radar layer).

## Page 8. fentale_rift_crack_1.jpg
What it is: a deep open fissure cutting through an old lava flow near Fentale, in Awash National Park.
Why it matters: this is a real rift crack from exactly the process that hit in 2025, ground pulling apart as magma pushes in underneath. It is not the 2025 railway crack itself, we could not find a licensed photo of that, but it is the honest substitute and it was taken a few kilometres away. If we cannot get a press photo of the railway, this opens the pitch.
Where: slide 1 candidate.

## Page 9. dofan_1.jpg
What it is: Dofan volcano from above, satellite view. Smaller image, public domain.
Why it matters: Dofan is the northern end of the 2025 dyke intrusion. Fentale and Dofan together frame the study area for the radar layer.
Where: slide 5, small inset next to Fentale.

## Page 10. east_african_rift_aster_1.jpg
What it is: a false colour image of part of the East African Rift from NASA's ASTER instrument on the Terra satellite. Deep fissures where the crust is splitting.
Why it matters: it is NASA data showing rifting, which ties our story directly to the datasets we are allowed to use. Good for the "Earth is tearing here" sentence.
Where: slide 2.

## Page 11. east_african_rift_gps_map_1.jpg
What it is: a map of the East African Rift system on a NASA SRTM elevation base, with arrows showing how fast each plate moves from GPS measurements. Nubian plate, Somali plate, Arabian plate, and the Afar triple junction where they meet.
Why it matters: this is the single clearest picture of why Afar is special. Three plates pulling apart at one point. It also justifies using SRTM, which is a NASA dataset.
Where: slide 2.

## Page 12. ethiopian_rift_map_1.jpg
What it is: a topographic map of the Ethiopian section of the Great Rift Valley, with the Ethiopian plateau, the Somali plateau, the Afar Depression and the rift lakes labelled.
Why it matters: it shows where Fentale, the rift lakes, the geothermal fields and Afar all sit on one line. Useful when you explain that the same process runs from Addis's doorstep up to Dallol.
Where: slide 2 or 3.

## Page 13. insar_kenya_rift_uplift_1.jpg
What it is: a radar interferogram from ESA's Envisat satellite showing volcanic uplift in the Kenyan section of the rift. The rainbow rings are centimetres of ground movement.
Why it matters: this is what our radar layer output will look like, and it is from the same rift system, just further south. Show this and say "this is what we will produce for Fentale."
Where: slide 5 (radar layer).

## Page 14. insar_volcano_usgs_1.jpg
What it is: a USGS interferogram of ground uplift at the Three Sisters volcanoes in Oregon, 1995 to 2001, shown as a 3D block.
Why it matters: a second, very clean example of InSAR detecting ground swelling from magma long before anything happened at the surface. Good for explaining the method to a non technical judge.
Where: slide 5 alternate, or the technical appendix slide if we need one.

## Page 15. jezero_panorama_1.jpg
What it is: a panorama of Jezero crater on Mars from the Perseverance rover, April 2021. The Ingenuity helicopter is visible in the shot.
Why it matters: Jezero is one of our two analog targets. Put this next to the Dallol or Danakil photo and the visual comparison does half the pitch for you.
Where: slide 4.

## Page 16. moon_south_pole_lro_1.jpg
What it is: a mosaic of the Moon's south pole from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter wide angle camera, about 600 km across. Shackleton crater is near the centre.
Why it matters: the second analog target. NASA's Artemis base plans are for this region.
Where: slide 4.

## Page 17. shackleton_ice_1.jpg
What it is: an LRO view of Shackleton crater with a colour overlay from the LOLA laser altimeter showing where researchers estimate ice sits in the permanently shadowed floor.
Why it matters: it shows the specific dimension (water ice, extreme cold, permanent shadow) that Earth analogs can only partly match. We use it on the honesty part of the scorecard: here is what Afar does not reproduce.
Where: slide 4, the "where the analogy breaks" point.

## Page 18. nisar_artist_1.jpg
What it is: NASA's artist concept of the NISAR satellite over Earth, with its 12 metre radar reflector deployed.
Why it matters: NISAR is the NASA and ISRO radar mission the "Dancing with the SARs" challenge is built around. Showing it tells the judges we know which mission our radar layer is meant to use.
Where: slide 5.

## Page 19. nisar_cleanroom_1.jpg
What it is: the real NISAR spacecraft in a clean room at ISRO in India after its main components were joined, with engineers around it.
Why it matters: a real photo rather than a rendering. Works as a credibility shot or on the data sources slide.
Where: slide 5 or slide 7.

## Page 20. cerberus_fossae_mars_1.jpg
What it is: an oblique view of Cerberus Fossae on Mars from ESA's Mars Express. Long parallel fissures cutting across the plains.
Why it matters: Cerberus Fossae is where NASA's InSight lander detected most of its marsquakes, and the fissures formed by the same mechanism as the Afar cracks, ground pulled apart by magma underneath. This is the picture that lets you say "Fentale in 2025 is what Cerberus Fossae looked like when it was forming." That sentence is the heart of the analog argument.
Where: slide 5, side by side with page 8.

## Page 21. afar_sentinel1_radar_semera_1.jpg
What it is: a Sentinel-1 radar image of Semera and the surrounding Afar lowlands, in false colour. Rough terrain shows bright, smooth surfaces dark.
Why it matters: this is actual radar data over our region. It shows the team what SAR imagery looks like before any processing and proves there is radar coverage of Afar.
Where: slide 5, or the data slide.

## Page 22. ethiopia_false_colour_esa_1.jpg
What it is: an ESA false colour satellite image of the rift lakes in central Ethiopia, the one ESA called "Ethiopian painting". Vegetation is red, water is dark.
Why it matters: it is beautiful and it is our rift. Good for a title or section divider.
Where: slide 3 or background.

## Page 23. firms_example_1.jpg
What it is: a screenshot of NASA's FIRMS fire map, in this case showing fires over Zealand in Denmark in 2024. Red and orange dots are thermal hotspots.
Why it matters: it is only here to show what the raw input to our fire layer looks like. The point of our layer is to turn twenty six years of dots like these into a calendar and to tell volcano heat apart from fire. Swap it for a FIRMS screenshot of Ethiopia once someone on the team makes one.
Where: slide 5, temporary.
